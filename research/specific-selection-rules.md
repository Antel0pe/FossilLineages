# Five specific selection rules that explain the most breadth of life

Follow-up to [`broad-selection-pressures.md`](broad-selection-pressures.md). That file
named the five broad pressure *categories* (predation, climate, resources, mates,
disease) — useful, but "climate is a selection pressure" isn't specific enough to
predict anything. This file drills one level deeper: for each category, what is the
actual **named, testable rule** — a specific input-shape-of-change relationship, not
just a category — and does that one rule keep reappearing in unrelated lineages that
never shared the problem at the same time or place?

Each rule below is stated as an if-then: a specific environmental input predicts a
specific anatomical/behavioral output. Same requirement as before: 5+ examples per
rule, pulled from taxa and eras that don't share ancestry on the trait in question.

---

## 1. Bergmann's / Allen's Rule — cold selects for bigger bodies and shorter limbs

**The rule:** in warm-blooded animals, sustained cold climate selects for larger overall
body size and shorter extremities (ears, limbs, tails); sustained heat selects for the
opposite. Mechanism in one link: surface-area-to-volume ratio controls heat loss, so a
bulkier, more compact body retains core heat while a leaner, larger-extremity body
sheds it — and individuals on the wrong side of that ratio for their climate lose more
energy to thermoregulation and leave fewer offspring.

1. **Arctic fox vs. fennec fox** — tiny, fur-buried ears and a compact rounded body
   (Arctic fox, Canada/Siberia) vs. ears nearly a third of body length (fennec fox,
   Sahara). Pressure: ear surface area is almost pure heat-loss real estate, so the
   optimal ear size flips depending on whether an animal needs to dump or keep heat.
2. **Neanderthals vs. equatorial Homo sapiens** — Neanderthals had a broader, barrel
   chest and notably shorter forearms and lower legs relative to their upper limbs
   than tropical-latitude modern human populations (e.g. Nilotic East Africans), who
   trend long-limbed and lean. Pressure: Ice Age Europe vs. equatorial Africa selected
   opposite ends of the same surface-area-to-volume dial within one genus.
3. **Emperor penguin vs. Galápagos penguin** — the emperor (Antarctic) is the largest
   living penguin at over 1m and 30kg; the Galápagos penguin (equatorial Pacific) is
   among the smallest at under half a meter. Pressure: same body plan, same order,
   opposite climates, opposite ends of the size range.
4. **White-tailed deer, mainland-to-Keys cline** — northern white-tailed deer (Canada,
   New England) run large; the Key deer subspecies confined to the warm Florida Keys
   is a natural dwarf, under a meter at the shoulder. Pressure: a single species shows
   the rule as a continuous cline along one latitude gradient, not just at the extremes.
5. **Woolly mammoth vs. Asian elephant** — the mammoth carried notably smaller ears and
   a thicker fat/fur layer than its closest living relative, despite both descending
   from a similar-bodied ancestor. Pressure: Ice Age steppe cold selected the mammoth
   lineage toward the low-surface-area end that its tropical-adapted cousin never
   needed.

## 2. Predator-prey speed/evasion arms race — pursuit pressure selects for escape ability, sometimes after the predator is gone

**The rule:** where prey is caught by direct pursuit, sustained predation selects for
faster sprint speed, tighter turning, or better evasive maneuvering in prey — and this
signal can persist for thousands of generations after the predator that caused it goes
extinct, because there's no "unselecting" a trait just because the pressure lifted.

1. **Cheetah vs. Thomson's gazelle** — both lineages independently became faster
   sprinters over millions of years. Pressure: each generation's fastest gazelles
   escaped and each generation's fastest cheetahs ate, so both ends of the interaction
   ratcheted upward together.
2. **Pronghorn antelope — "ghost of predators past"** — pronghorn can sustain speeds
   near 90 km/h, far faster than any predator alive in North America today. Pressure:
   that speed evolved to escape the American cheetah (Miracinonyx), which went extinct
   roughly 12,000 years ago — the trait is still there because there was never a
   predator slow enough to select it back down.
3. **Trinidadian guppies (experimentally verified)** — guppies transplanted from
   low-predation to high-predation streams show measurably faster burst-swimming speed
   and sharper evasive turns within a few dozen generations, and the reverse when
   moved the other way. Pressure: this is one of the few cases where the arms race has
   been directly observed running in real time, in both directions.
4. **Snowshoe hare vs. lynx** — hares run in tight zig-zags rather than straight lines,
   trading top speed for a turning radius tighter than a pursuing lynx or coyote can
   match. Pressure: against a predator that's often faster in a straight line,
   maneuverability beats raw speed.
5. **Flying fish escaping pelagic predators (tuna, dolphinfish, mackerel)** — enlarged
   pectoral fins let them break the surface and glide 50+ meters over open water.
   Pressure: underwater speed alone couldn't outrun faster ocean predators, so the
   escape route shifted to a medium (air) the predator couldn't follow into.
6. **Springbok and gazelle stotting (pronking)** — a stiff-legged, vertical leaping
   display performed directly in view of an approaching predator, at the cost of a
   head start. Pressure: an honest fitness signal — only an animal with speed to spare
   can afford to stot — that causes predators to abandon the chase and target a
   weaker individual instead.

## 3. The Island Rule — isolation with limited resources shrinks big lineages and grows small ones

**The rule:** on islands (or other resource-capped, low-competition, low-predator
isolates), large-bodied lineages trend toward dwarfism and small-bodied lineages trend
toward gigantism. Mechanism in one link: a fixed, limited food supply can't support a
mainland-sized population of a large animal, while a small animal on a
predator-depleted island no longer pays the usual cost of getting bigger.

1. **Homo floresiensis, Flores, Indonesia** — a hominin standing roughly 1.1m tall,
   descended from a larger-bodied Homo erectus population. Pressure: isolation on a
   resource-limited island with no competing large hominins selected for a smaller,
   more energy-efficient body over many generations.
2. **Mediterranean dwarf elephants (Sicily, Malta, Crete, Cyprus)** — Palaeoloxodon
   elephants standing around 1m at the shoulder, descended from ~4m mainland ancestors.
   Pressure: multiple separate island populations of the same genus independently
   shrank to a similar size ceiling set by each island's food supply.
3. **Wrangel Island dwarf mammoths** — the last surviving woolly mammoths, isolated on
   Wrangel Island until roughly 4,000 years ago, ran smaller than mainland mammoths.
   Pressure: the same size-capping mechanism as the Mediterranean elephants, on the
   opposite side of the planet and in a different genus.
4. **Komodo dragon, Indonesian islands** — the largest living lizard, over 3m long, on
   islands with no large mammalian predators or competitors. Pressure: the small side
   of the rule — without bigger predators to stay small and hidden from, and with
   large prey (deer, water buffalo) available, size became an advantage instead of a
   liability.
5. **Galápagos and Aldabra giant tortoises** — reptiles that grew far larger than
   mainland tortoise relatives on predator-free, isolated islands. Pressure: without
   predation pressure keeping body size down, size became mainly a thermal and
   competitive advantage instead of a cost.
6. **Giant wētā, New Zealand** — flightless insects that grew to among the largest and
   heaviest in the world, in an island ecosystem historically without native land
   mammals. Pressure: the same predator-vacuum and niche-filling dynamic as the Komodo
   dragon, in an insect instead of a reptile.

## 4. Runaway sexual selection — the more reproductive skew a mating system has, the more exaggerated the ornament/weapon gets

**The rule:** where a small fraction of males father most of the offspring (high
reproductive skew — leks, harems, intense male-male combat), female choice and
male-male competition escalate a chosen trait past what pure survival would ever favor,
because the reproductive payoff for winning outweighs the survival cost of carrying it.

1. **Peacock tail, Indian peafowl** — an enormous, metabolically costly train that
   makes flight harder and predator attention worse. Pressure: peahens consistently
   choose the largest, most symmetrical trains, so the trait kept escalating past any
   point that predation or energy cost alone would allow.
2. **Irish elk antlers (Megaloceros giganteus, extinct)** — antlers spanning up to
   3.5m, the largest of any known deer, far past what's useful for fighting alone.
   Pressure: both male combat and female preference for the largest rack pushed antler
   size into a runaway trajectory that outpaced the animal's ability to carry it.
3. **Northern elephant seal** — males reach roughly 3-4x female body mass, fighting for
   control of beach harems where a handful of "beachmaster" males father most of a
   season's pups. Pressure: reproductive skew this steep makes any survival cost of
   extra size worth paying for the chance to be one of the winners.
4. **Stalk-eyed flies (Diopsidae)** — eye stalks that in some species exceed the fly's
   own body length, driven by female preference for wide eye-span. Pressure: this is
   one of the few ornaments with a known, trackable genetic basis (X-linked), so the
   runaway process has been mapped down to specific alleles.
5. **Long-tailed widowbird (experimentally verified)** — a 1982 field experiment
   (Andersson) that artificially lengthened some males' tails and shortened others'
   found mating success tracked tail length directly, in both directions. Pressure:
   direct experimental proof that the ornament itself, not some other correlated
   trait, drives female choice.
6. **Birds-of-paradise, New Guinea** — dozens of closely related species with wildly
   different, elaborate plumage and courtship dances, radiating from a comparatively
   plain ancestor. Pressure: lek-based mating where males display and females choose
   with no male parental investment lets ornamentation diverge fast and freely between
   otherwise similar species.

## 5. Pathogen-driven selective sweep — a novel disease selects fast, heritable resistance in survivors within a few generations

**The rule:** when a population meets a new, highly lethal pathogen it has no prior
immunity to, mortality is so steep that any pre-existing genetic variant conferring
even partial resistance sweeps through the surviving population within a small number
of generations — far faster than "typical" adaptation, because the selection
differential (die vs. survive) is close to absolute.

1. **Sickle cell trait and malaria (sub-Saharan Africa)** — a hemoglobin mutation that
   distorts red blood cells persists at high frequency despite causing disease in
   double-copy carriers. Pressure: single-copy carriers resist malaria well enough
   that the gene's benefit outweighs its cost across the population, even though it's
   harmful to some individuals.
2. **Australian rabbits and myxomatosis (1950s)** — after the myxoma virus killed
   roughly 99% of Australia's introduced rabbit population, measurable genetic
   resistance appeared in survivors' descendants within about two decades. Pressure:
   near-total mortality compressed what would normally take centuries into a handful
   of rabbit generations.
3. **Tasmanian devils and devil facial tumor disease (1990s-present)** — a
   transmissible cancer that killed most devils in affected populations; several
   populations now show rising frequency of alleles linked to tumor regression and
   younger, earlier breeding age. Pressure: an ongoing, currently-trackable sweep,
   still being measured population by population.
4. **Mountain yellow-legged frogs and chytrid fungus (Sierra Nevada)** — populations
   that survived the initial chytrid die-off show skin microbiome and immune-gene
   differences absent in naive populations, and some are rebounding. Pressure: the
   same near-total-mortality sweep dynamic, in an amphibian and a fungal pathogen
   instead of a mammal and a virus.
5. **Little brown bats and white-nose syndrome (North America, 2006-present)** — a
   fungal disease that has killed millions of bats, with some surviving colonies now
   showing altered hibernation behavior and physiological markers associated with
   resistance. Pressure: another currently-unfolding sweep, in a flying mammal, giving
   a fourth independent lineage running the same rule at the same time as devils and
   frogs.
6. **CCR5-Δ32 allele in European populations (humans)** — a deletion mutation that
   blocks a receptor used by certain pathogens to enter immune cells reached
   unusually high frequency in Europe, plausibly driven by historical epidemics
   (smallpox and/or plague are the leading candidates). Pressure: the same mechanism
   as the other five, in a human population, over a longer and less directly observed
   timescale.

## Why these five, together

Each is a specific, directional if-then rule rather than a category label, which means
each one is falsifiable on sight in a new species: does cold correlate with bigger
bodies and shorter limbs in this lineage or not; is this island population smaller or
bigger than its mainland relatives; did resistance alleles rise in frequency after the
pathogen arrived or not. The five together still span the same five independent axes
as the broader pressures they refine — thermal physics, pursuit dynamics, resource
ceilings, reproductive competition, and pathogen lethality — which is why the same
five rules keep re-deriving themselves independently across mammals, birds, reptiles,
fish, insects, and humans that share no recent common solution to the problem.
