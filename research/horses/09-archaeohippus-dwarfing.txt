PANEL 9 — Branch point (third sibling): Miohippus -> Archaeohippus dwarfing
~21-13 Ma — a third sibling alongside Panel 2's Anchitheriinae and Equinae-stem branches

BASELINE
Panel 2 established Miohippus (roughly 50-120 lbs) splitting into a browsing sibling
(Anchitheriinae, stayed the same size and spread across two continents) and a grazing-stem
sibling (Equinae stem, grew taller teeth for a grittier diet). Almost every other lineage on
this page after this point trends toward bigger bodies over time.

WHAT CHANGED + WHY
What changed: Archaeohippus, appearing around 21 Ma and persisting to about 13 Ma, went the
opposite direction from both of its Miohippus-descended siblings: instead of staying the same
size (Anchitheriinae) or trending toward the general size-up pattern most of the family
followed, its body shrank to roughly the size of a collie dog — a real, sustained size
reduction documented across multiple named species and localities, not a single small
individual [34][35]. Tooth crown height stayed low, like the Anchitheriinae sibling, marking
Archaeohippus as a browser rather than a grazer [35].
Why: against its bigger-bodied grazing cousins spreading onto the newly open ground nearby,
going smaller — not bigger — let Archaeohippus specialize on a food supply (soft leaves in
dense forest/woodland understory) that a larger body's size and metabolic needs made a poor
fit for, opening a niche that its own larger relatives, Miohippus included, could not use as
efficiently.

WHY-OF-THE-WHY
The most detailed dedicated study of this lineage's dwarfing (O'Sullivan's work on
Archaeohippus, including a chapter specifically titled "Dwarfing and the Gulf Coastal Plain")
links the size reduction to a specific region — the well-preserved Archaeohippus fossils
central to this story come from Florida/Gulf Coastal Plain sites, a part of the continent that
plausibly retained wetter, more forested conditions for longer than the Great Plains interior
undergoing the grassland transition described in Panel 2. That would make Archaeohippus's
dwarfing a story tied to a specific surviving pocket of forest habitat, not a continent-wide
trend — but the full text of that source was not accessible beyond its chapter titles and
summary, so this geographic link should be read as the author's own apparent thesis, not
independently verified here.

CONFIDENCE
High on the fact of dwarfing itself: real, sustained, documented across multiple species and
sites, running against the size-up trend the rest of the family followed over the same
interval [3][34]. Lower than the other panels on this page on the specific why: the
forest-understory/resource-partitioning explanation is the standard, reasonable reading of the
tooth and body-size evidence, but the two most specific dedicated sources on Archaeohippus's
dwarfing (O'Sullivan's dissertation work and a related body-size-evolution chapter) were only
reachable as abstracts/summaries in this research pass, not full text — flag this as the
weakest-sourced why-of-the-why among the panels researched here, not a settled mechanism.
