PANEL 8 — Branch point: early Equus/Dinohippus lineage -> crown Equus vs. Haringtonhippus
(New World stilt-legged horse) vs. Hippidion (South American horse)
~7-4 Ma North American split; Hippidion reaches South America ~2.7-2.5 Ma; both non-crown
branches extinct by the end of the last Ice Age

This replaces an older, now-superseded textbook story. For decades Hippidion was classified as
a descendant of Pliohippus specifically, on the strength of a similarly-shaped tooth (an oval
protocone) [33]. Ancient DNA has since overturned that.

BASELINE
Panel 4 established that by ~4-6 Ma, the one-toed grazing Equus lineage (through Dinohippus)
was established in North America — the continent the whole horse family evolved on. What
wasn't visible from bones alone is that this same general population, over roughly the next
2-3 million years, split into three lineages rather than one.

WHAT CHANGED + WHY — Haringtonhippus (New World stilt-legged horse) vs. crown Equus
What changed: one branch kept a stouter, Equus-like build and became the ancestor of every
living horse, zebra, and ass (already covered in the Equus-branches panel). A separate branch
— genetically distinct enough that ancient-DNA researchers gave it its own new genus,
Haringtonhippus, in 2017 — had slender, stilt-like lower leg bones and narrower hooves,
unlike stouter Equus [31]. For over a century its fossils (originally named as Equus species,
E. francisci and E. calobatus) were simply filed as odd, slim-legged species of Equus, because
skeletally it looks like a slender Equus. Its mitochondrial genome carries 178 diagnostic
differences from Equus, including four insertions — different enough that it wasn't an
unusual Equus species but a wholly separate genus that had been quietly coexisting alongside
true Equus across Ice Age North America the entire time, without merging into the Equus gene
pool [31].
Why: a slender, long-limbed build shows up independently in Old World asses/hemiones facing
similar terrain — the standard functional reading of that shape is efficient, low-cost travel
across wide, resource-sparse, arid ground, as against the stockier build suited to Equus's
grazing lifestyle.
Terminal fact: Haringtonhippus persisted through the whole Ice Age and died out only at its
very end — radiocarbon-dated to as late as ~14,400 years ago in the Yukon/Alaska region and
~13,100 years ago in Nevada — in the same general end-Pleistocene window as most of North
America's other Ice Age megafauna [31].

WHAT CHANGED + WHY — Hippidion (South American horse)
What changed: a related lineage dispersed south once the Isthmus of Panama closed the water
gap around 2.7-3 Ma, and there developed the classic Hippidion build: short, heavy leg bones —
read as an adaptation for stability on the sloped, uneven ground of the Andean margin, rather
than for fast travel across flat, sparse terrain like its North American stilt-legged
relative. Against Haringtonhippus's long, slender legs, Hippidion's short, stout legs are the
opposite locomotor trade-off — stability over efficiency — for a genuinely different kind of
terrain.
Why it was misclassified: Hippidion's teeth resemble Pliohippus's, and its stout legs were
read as a shared, inherited trait from Pliohippus [33]. Complete ancient mitochondrial genomes
overturned this: Hippidion actually sits as an outgroup to every living Equus lineage,
branching off from within the Equus side of the family tree entirely, around 5.6-7.7 million
years ago — well after Pliohippus and the Equus lineage had already parted ways — not
descended from Pliohippus at all [32]. Genome-scale data further places Hippidion's branch-off
point slightly earlier than, and separate from, Haringtonhippus's own split from Equus: it is
Haringtonhippus's more distant cousin, not its direct sister, and both are more closely
related to living Equus than either is to Pliohippus [31].

WHY-OF-THE-WHY
Why did skeleton-based classification get both of these lineages wrong, in the same direction,
for decades? This isn't a one-off case of missing data — it's a documented, repeating pattern
in equid paleontology: superficially similar traits (a particular tooth shape, a particular
limb proportion) evolve convergently more than once across unrelated horse lineages facing
similar mechanical demands, and equid ancient-DNA work has now overturned skeleton-based
classification for multiple, separate parts of the horse family tree using this same
technique — not just Hippidion's relationship to Pliohippus, but Haringtonhippus's own status
as a distinct genus rather than an odd Equus species [31][33]. The tooth and limb shapes
weren't wrong observations; they just weren't the reliable signal of ancestry they were once
assumed to be.

CONFIDENCE
High on the corrected phylogeny itself: Hippidion as an outgroup to the Haringtonhippus+Equus
clade, and Haringtonhippus as a genuinely separate genus more closely related to Equus than
Hippidion is, is now the consistent finding across independent ancient-DNA studies (Der
Sarkissian et al. 2015 on mitochondrial genomes; Heintzman et al. 2017 on mitochondrial and
nuclear genomes) — this supersedes the older, Pliohippus-based morphological classification.
Moderate on the specific terrain explanation for why each lineage's limbs went the direction
they did: the stilt-legged/short-legged functional readings are the standard, plausible
inference from limb proportions and Old World parallels, but no dedicated biomechanical or
paleoclimate study was found pinning either build to a tested mechanism specific to this
exact split — treat that part as reasonable inference, not settled fact.
