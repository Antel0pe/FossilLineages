PANEL 6 — Straight line: Mesohippus -> Miohippus
Late Eocene into early Oligocene (~36-32 Ma) — sits between Panel 1 (Hyracotherium ->
Mesohippus) and Panel 2 (Miohippus branches)

BASELINE
Mesohippus (Panel 1) was a small, three-toed woodland browser — roughly 50-75 lbs — with a
shallow facial fossa (a hollow pit on the face), living across a patchy North American
woodland that continued cooling and drying through the late Eocene into the early Oligocene.
It held this basic size and shape with little change for several million years.

WHAT CHANGED + WHY
What changed: Miohippus did not gradually grow out of Mesohippus one generation at a time.
A Miohippus population split off from Mesohippus as a separate lineage and then ran alongside
it for roughly four million years — multiple species of both genera are found together in the
same rock layers at the same sites (e.g. Lusk, Wyoming), which is direct physical evidence of
a branching event, not a data gap papered over by "few transitional fossils" [27]. The
Miohippus population was distinctly larger-bodied than Mesohippus — on the order of 120 lbs
against Mesohippus's 50-75 lbs — and had a noticeably bigger, deeper facial fossa [27][28].
Against the Mesohippus baseline, which had held roughly the same small size for millions of
years beforehand, this was a fast, discrete step up in body size tied to a new, separate
population, not a slow drift.
Why: A genuine size jump of this kind, in a herbivore living through the same continuing
cooling/opening-woodland trend already driving Panel 1's changes, plausibly worked the same
way it does in living ungulates moving into patchier, more open ground: a bigger body is
harder for most local predators to bring down, and it can go longer between food patches when
the good browse is more scattered. This is the standard ecological reading of the size jump,
not a dedicated finding pinned to this specific transition — see Confidence below.

WHY-OF-THE-WHY
The "abrupt, cladogenetic, coexisting" pattern is not unique to this one pair of genera. The
same statistical/morphometric revision that established it (Prothero & Shubin's restudy of the
White River Oligocene sequence) found the same shape of pattern — long stasis within a species,
punctuated by branching events, rather than one line smoothly morphing into the next —
repeated across several other contemporaneous equid lineages in the same beds [27]. That
independent, broader pattern across multiple lineages is what gives paleontologists confidence
this is a real branching event and not just a gap in an otherwise gradual, poorly-sampled
transformation — the opposite of how the popular "few transitional fossils" phrasing can read.

CONFIDENCE
High on the fact and shape of the transition itself: cladogenetic origin, ~4-million-year
stratigraphic overlap, and the size/facial-fossa differences are all directly documented, and
Wikipedia's soft "relatively abrupt... few transitional fossils" phrasing actually understates
how solid this finding is — it's a positive, well-evidenced branching pattern, not an
evidence gap. Moderate on exactly why body size specifically jumped: the
patchier-terrain/predator-evasion explanation is the standard ecological inference for this
kind of ungulate size increase, but unlike several later panels on this page (which lean on a
dedicated dietary or isotopic study), no single study pins the size increase itself to one
tested mechanism — treat that half of the "why" as a reasonable inference, not a settled
finding. The precise fine-grained phylogenetic detail of the broader early equid tree is
separately acknowledged in the systematics literature as less resolved than later parts of the
tree [8].
