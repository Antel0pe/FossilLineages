PANEL 7 — Dead end: Anchitheriinae's own extinction (Anchitherium, Sinohippus, Hypohippus,
Megahippus)
Split from Miohippus ~32-24 Ma (Panel 2); last lineages extinct by the end of the Miocene
(~9-8.8 Ma)

This panel is not a foil for the Equinae-stem sibling. Anchitheriinae ran as a genuinely
successful browsing lineage for roughly 20+ million years across two continents before it
ended — its extinction has its own, only partly resolved, causal story.

BASELINE
Panel 2 established the split: Miohippus's Anchitheriinae descendants kept the ancestral
three-toed foot and low-crowned, browsing teeth essentially unchanged, while its Equinae-stem
sibling began raising tooth crown height to handle a grittier, grassier diet. Anchitherium
itself dispersed out of North America across Asia and Europe by ~18-19 Ma, and in both
regions gave rise to larger-bodied descendants that kept the same browsing body plan:
Hypohippus and Megahippus in North America, Sinohippus in Eurasia.

WHAT CHANGED + TERMINAL FACT
What changed: essentially nothing about the core body plan changed further. Hypohippus and
Megahippus grew pony- to draft-horse-sized bodies but kept the low-crowned teeth and
closed-habitat-suited limb proportions of their Anchitherium ancestors, and direct fossil
evidence from a Eurasian site (Sandelzhausen, Germany) shows Anchitherium living there
alongside other browsers in a still-wooded community, not adapting toward any grassland diet
[9]. Against their Equinae-stem/hipparionine cousins — who kept adjusting tooth height and
eventually built fully grassland-ready teeth and feet (Panels 2-4) — the anchitheriines never
changed their basic strategy across their entire run.
Terminal fact: Hypohippus and Megahippus persisted through the whole "Clarendonian
chronofauna" interval of relative ecosystem stability and went extinct only at its close, in
the late Miocene [30]. Old World Anchitherium/Sinohippus lineages disappear on a similar
general timescale. By the end of the Miocene, no anchitheriine lineage on either continent
remained.

WHY
An independent, quantitative fossil-record study across 37 North American Great Plains
localities found that browsing-ungulate species richness (a category anchitheriine horses
were a major, classic part of) didn't collapse in one event — it declined steadily and
continuously from a peak around 19 Ma down to modern-comparable low levels by roughly 10 Ma
[29]. That is a long-term habitat/resource squeeze playing out over millions of years, not a
sudden displacement — the last anchitheriines died out only once that long decline had nearly
run its full course.

WHY-OF-THE-WHY
The same study's own proposed driver for the browse decline is a genuinely separate evidence
stream from the grassland-paleosol story already used in Panel 2: it points to falling
atmospheric CO2 through the Miocene reducing C3 (leafy-plant) primary productivity, using a
modern rainfall/productivity-to-diversity correlation in African savannas as its comparative
baseline, rather than direct grass-cover mapping [29]. That same paper also directly
complicates the tidy "grazers out-competed browsers" story that anchitheriine extinction is
often casually explained by: it explicitly found the decline in browser richness was not a
one-for-one replacement by grazing horses, meaning the data does not support direct
competitive exclusion as the clean mechanism it's often assumed to be.

CONFIDENCE
The timing (steady browser decline ~19-10 Ma; final anchitheriine genera extinct at the close
of the Clarendonian, late Miocene) is well established from direct fossil-locality data. The
cause is only partly resolved: the leading quantitative study points to a CO2/productivity-
driven browse scarcity and explicitly argues against simple grazer competition as the
mechanism — but that study is about browsing ungulates broadly, not a dedicated study isolating
anchitheriine horses specifically from the other browsers that declined alongside them. Treat
"habitat/productivity squeeze vs. direct competition" here as a real, still-open weighting
question, not a settled ranking — the same honesty this project applies to the Hipparionini
extinction (Panel 5).
