high level photosynthesis uses the energy from photons to pull low energy electrons from oxygen and put them into higher energy carbon bonds. this stores the energy. when we want to extract the energy we move the electrons from the carbon back into oxygen bonds which releases the stored energy.

## catching the photon
an electron can only absorb the energy of a photon if the photon contains the exact amount of energy to move the electron from its current position to another valid position in the molecule. if its more/less - nothing happens. 

chlorophyll has a structure called a porphyrin ring that positions electrons such that visible light has the exact amount of energy to push the electrons into another stable position.

however, each individual chlorophyll molecule doesn't get hit often. so what plants do is make a funnel of chlorophyll molecules to catch the photon energy and funnel it into a central area. 

the keyword is that the energy is transferred, not the electron. 

in order for energy to be transferred through FRET (Forster Resonance Energy Transfer), the receiving electron must be at a lower energy than the giving electron. in addition they must also be close enough, otherwise the energy will be dissipated as heat/light.

## photosystem 2

the energy travels until it reaches the reaction center. this is called photosystem 2 (ps2). 2 because it was discovered second but is actually the first step.

there's a long list of molecules that do the transferring like P680, pheophytin, plastoquinone but for the purpose of this they aren't important. all we care about is there are a chain of molecules that move electrons/energy.

after the energy reaches the reaction center, the energy stops being transferred via fields and starts being transferred via yanked electrons.

one of the molecules that gets its electron yanked is p680. after getting yanked, it is p680+. 

p680+ is one of the strongest electron stealers in biology and is one of the few things that can rip an electron away from water which is an incredibly hard thing to do. 

the pulling happens via an oxygen evolving complex (oec). essentially 2 water molecules are held in place, p680 has its electron stolen 4 times, and in turn steals 4 electrons from water. the protons and O2 from water are then released.

## after ps 2
p680 repeatedly has its electron stolen, gets another one, gets stolen again, etc. these stolen electrons travel down to plastoquinone (pq).

pq picks up 2 electrons then attracts 2 protons from the stroma to balance the charge.

pq then moves through a membrane into the lumen and drops the electrons and protons off on the other side

## atp synthase
the atp synthase produces ATP which provides energy for important things down the line.

ATP is produced by ADP and a phosphate getting together. the atp synthase clamps the 2 together and gets them to bond. once they've bonded it's really hard to get the synthase to release them.

this is where the other portion of the synthase comes into play. 

remember in the previous section where pq attracts 2 protons, moves into the lumen, and drops the protons off? this results in a proton concentration difference between the stroma and the lumen. 

as the protons rush from the lumen into the stroma through a tiny tunnel in the synthase, it turns a shaft which  scrapes the atp off of the enzyme. 

## photosystem 1
photosystem 1 (ps1) is similar in structure to ps2 with molecules capturing photon energy and a funnel towards the reaction center. 

the electron travelling down from ps2 into ps1 is significantly less energized than it was. so ps1 captures a second photon to re-energize it. 

this electron is then donated to NADP+ which turns into NADPH. this is the second crucial component needed later on with ATP. 

at this point we have used light + water to create ATP, NADPH, and O2 as waste. ATP and NADPH are integral components of the calvin cycle

## calvin cycle
the calvin cycle is about taking the NADPH, ATP, and a molecule called RuBP and turning them into useful energy stores like glucose and cellulose. 

Rubisco is an enzyme that holds RuBP. The carbon from a CO2 and H2O are attached to RuBP. This creates 2 3-PGA molecules. 

The ATP we produced earlier donates a phosphate group to the 3-PGA. This phosphate group picks up an oxygen and then drifts away. NADPH then delivers a hydride, proton with 2 electrons. This turns the 3-PGA into a G3P which can be turned into glucose and other useful materials.

The last step is regenerating the RuBP which is a key input to this cycle. Most of the G3P produced is used to regenerate the RuBP that was used up. This also requires an additional ATP.

In total we have 3 RuBP that is used to make 6 G3P. 5 of the 6 G3P are used to rebuild the 3 RuBP. The remaining G3P leaves as profit.

Throughout this process we used 6 ATP and 6 NADPH to produce 6 G3P. In addition it took 3 ATP to regenerate the 3 RuBP. In total 9 ATP and 6 NADPH to produce 1 profit G3P. 

This imbalance of ATP vs NADPH needed is why there are systems in the photosystems to loop electrons around to be re-energized to run the ATP producing cycle again rather than continuing directly from start to finish. 

## the end
Plants and animals produce energy from sugars like glucose by running this loop in reverse. We strip electrons off carbon, let them fall back onto oxygen, and capture the energy.

We have many of the same structures like the ATP synthase.
