# Candidate lineages for the branch/evolution-point treatment (non-human)

Not a skill yet — scouting only. Filter applied (per user, 2026-07-08): deep time with real
speciation/diversification into multiple descendant branches, not within-population trait
oscillation over a few decades (the reason **Grant's finches** — `app/lab/`, ~40-year record on
Daphne Major — is an explicit non-example here: real and well-evidenced, but no lineage
splitting, just one population's trait wobbling under alternating pressure). Also weighted toward
lineages with unusually good, already-synthesized fossil + habitat + causal literature, since
that's the actual bottleneck this project keeps hitting (see `notes.md`).

Ranked by fit. None of this has been built — treat every "why it fits" line as a hypothesis to
re-verify (real sources, real numbers) at build time, not a settled fact.

## 1. Horses (Equidae) — top pick
- ~55 million years, overwhelmingly North America-centered, so the fossil sampling is unusually
  dense and continuous (not scattered across continents like hominins).
- Genuine adaptive radiation: Hyracotherium/"Eohippus" → Mesohippus → Merychippus → Pliohippus →
  Equus, with dozens of side-branch genera that went extinct, not a straight ladder.
- Has a direct analog to the boisei/robustus isotope story already mined for this project: the
  Miocene C3-woodland-browser → C4-grassland-grazer transition is isotopically documented in
  tooth enamel, giving a real comparative "why" (who stayed a browser, who committed to grazing,
  and what each one's teeth/legs show for it).
- Only one genus (Equus) survives out of a much larger radiation — same "most branches didn't
  make it" texture as the hominin graph, without needing to invent an outcome bias.
- Caveat: it's a very famous "textbook" example — worth checking it doesn't feel over-familiar
  compared to the surprise value hominin siblings currently deliver.

## 2. Whales (Cetacea)
- ~50 million years, land-to-sea transition with an unusually complete transitional series
  (Pakicetus → Ambulocetus → Rodhocetus → Basilosaurus/Dorudon → modern whales), concentrated at
  a few extraordinary sites (Pakistan, Wadi Al-Hitan in Egypt).
- Hard-evidence density is very high: oxygen-isotope enamel data directly tracks the freshwater→
  marine shift, and hind-limb reduction is documented skeleton by skeleton.
- Real diversification with a clean two-way fork: odontocetes (echolocation, toothed) vs.
  mysticetes (baleen filter-feeding) — different feeding strategies, different skulls, dateable
  divergence.
- Caveat: the land-to-sea run is so dramatic it may pull toward turning-point-scene format more
  than the comparative branch-point format this project's graph currently uses — worth deciding
  which container fits before starting.

## 3. Proboscideans (elephants, mammoths, mastodons)
- ~60 million years from Moeritherium, large radiation (deinotheres, gomphotheres, stegodons,
  mammoths, mastodons) tied closely to habitat/climate shifts.
- More branch points than horses or whales — more raw material for pairwise "why did these two
  siblings diverge" comparisons.
- Caveat: the most famous "why" question about this lineage — why almost all of it went extinct
  — is the climate-vs-human-overkill debate, which is contested among specialists (same shape as
  the paleoclimate-attribution caution already on file for this project). The habitat/diet
  divergence content earlier in each branch's life stands on its own regardless; treat the
  end-of-lineage extinction cause as a "here's the live debate," not a settled causal line.

## 4. Canids (dog/wolf/fox lineage)
- ~40 million years, three successive North American radiations (Hesperocyoninae →
  Borophaginae → Caninae), with borophagine "bone-crushing" hypercarnivores as a dead-end branch
  against the surviving generalist canine line — another real "most branches didn't make it"
  story.
- Caveat: thinner public-facing synthesis than horses/whales; the core literature (Wang &
  Tedford) is more specialist/technical, so more up-front digestion work before it's writable at
  this project's bar.

## 5. Camelidae (camels, llamas, alpacas)
- ~45 million years, originated and diversified almost entirely in North America — a genuinely
  surprising fact most readers don't know, with real hook value (comparable to the "gorillas and
  humans share a branch point" surprise already working in this project).
- Dispersed to South America (llamas/alpacas, via the Great American Biotic Interchange) and Old
  World (dromedary/bactrian, via Beringia) before going extinct in its own homeland — a clean,
  concrete divergence-then-differential-persistence story.
- Caveat: smaller, less mainstream fossil literature than the top three — check evidence density
  before committing.

## Not shortlisted, and why
- **Ammonites / other shelled marine invertebrates**: fossil record is excellent but soft-tissue
  behavior/habitat evidence is thin — the "why" would lean more on inference than the hard,
  multi-line evidence this project's bar wants.
- **Trilobites**: same shape — great record of *what*, weak record of *why* per lineage split.
- **Anything with a single well-known extinction event as its whole story (e.g. ammonites at the
  K-Pg boundary)**: already covered by `app/lab/` (Chicxulub explainer) in a different format;
  redundant as a new lineage pick.
